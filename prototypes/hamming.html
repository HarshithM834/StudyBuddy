CYSE 425 · Hamming (7,4)

How three checks find one flipped bit.

Follow the exact ABCD | XYZ layout from your lecture, one step at a time.

Step 1 of 4

We want to send four bits: 1101.

A bit is a 0 or a 1. The slide names the four message positions A, B, C, D.

A1
B1
C0
D1

Suppose noise changes C from 0 to 1. The receiver gets 1111. That looks like a perfectly possible message. How could it know something changed?

Send extra information that lets the receiver check the message. Hamming (7,4) adds three check bits: four message bits + three checks = seven transmitted bits.

This lesson assumes at most one of those seven bits changes during transmission. Ordinary Hamming (7,4) cannot reliably correct two flipped bits.

Step 2 of 4

Each check looks at a different group.

The symbol ⊕ means XOR. For these groups, count the 1s: an odd count gives 1; an even count gives 0.

CheckMessage bits it usesWith ABCD = 1101Result
XA, B, D1 ⊕ 1 ⊕ 1 → three 1s1
YA, C, D1 ⊕ 0 ⊕ 1 → two 1s0
ZB, C, D1 ⊕ 0 ⊕ 1 → two 1s0
We send 1101 | 100.
The first four bits carry the message. The last three carry the results of our checks.

The groups are chosen so each bit has a unique pattern of checks. That pattern will let us locate a single error.
